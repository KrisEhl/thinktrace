Zohm3 1Karlsruhe Institute for Technology (KIT) 2Forschungszentrum Jülich (FZJ) 3Max-Planck-Institut für Plasmaphysik (IPP), Garching and Greifswald Introduction The qualification of fusion nuclear technologies for deployment in future reactors is a critical step towards the realization of fusion power. It also forms a key element in the licensing process, ensuring the safety, performance, and reliability of fusion nuclear systems.
Particularly for the Breeding Blanket (BB), a first step to validate the performance of a blanket concept under the nominal operational conditions is performed, as part of the qualification process.
Afterwards, a foundational aspect of the qualification, is the systematic identification and evaluation of failure modes that are design drivers and which uncertainties remain significant, mostly due to lack of experimental data under fusion neutrons and cycling heat loads. Each of these critical failure modes carries a risk, which are to be addressed by dedicated tests within a qualification strategy.
Given the complexity and performance demands of BB technologies, their development requires sustained and focused R&D efforts. Several key studies conducted in the 1980s and 1990s, such as the FINESSE1 and CORIANDER2 studies, identified major knowledge gaps and R&D needs for BB development. These studies have been partially revisited in light of new design concepts and the availability of testing infrastructure, including ITER’s Test Blanket Modules (TBM), IFMIF-DONES, and other neutron sources possibly under development in Europe.
The present EU Roadmap for the Realization of Fusion Energy3 is built on 2 pillars: (1) ITER, which should have provided plasma performance up to Q=10 and, in parallel, provide information on the Breeding Blanket performance and reliability by testing Test Blanket Modules (TBM) and (2) DONES, a neutron source which shall qualify structural materials. However, due to the reduction of the neutron fluence in the DT-1 phase in the new baseline and further delays in the ITER project, the TBM programme has been forced to reduce its scope. This descoping and the new project schedule of ITER forces the fusion community to revise the qualification strategy of the breeding blanket (and other critical in-vessel components). For this, it is critical to revise the past work on the qualification needs, to clarify each facility’s role in the technology maturation ladder and to identify possible critical gaps.

Table 1: Possible Failure Modes in the Breeding Blanket Requirements for Breeding Blanket Testing and Qualification Given the broad spectrum of failure modes possible in the Breeding Blanket and due to their central role in a fusion power plant, it is clear that these must undergo a rigorous testing and qualification process to ensure safe, reliable, and efficient operation over their lifetime. These tests must aim to understand the complex, interrelated physical processes which can lead to the aforementioned failure modes. Above all the loads, neutron irradiation is indispensable in most experiments, as it replicates the fusion environment, especially regarding heating, tritium production, and material degradation.
Ten different families of experiments can be identified in order to classify the types of testing that are necessary to cover the failure modes in Table 2. These families have been defined in increasing level of complexity, from small scale, single-effect tests, to engineering-scale, combined and multiplier-effect testing.
This increase in complexity maps the technology maturation roadmap given by the Technology Readiness Levels, as it will be explained in the next section.

Qualification Strategy Testing and qualifying a breeding blanket requires a diverse set of carefully controlled experiments, each replicating a specific operational condition of a fusion reactor. Neutron irradiation is a key factor across all test types. From small unit cells to full-scale modules, each experiment helps validate a critical part of the BB's design – from heat and stress handling to tritium management and structural longevity. The primary goal of the BB R&D Program should be to systematically assess risks associated to potential BB failure modes evaluating their impact on safety, reliability, and/or performance. This risk-based approach will support then the prioritization of design improvements, targeted testing, and mitigation strategies.
In this context, the Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs) provide a practical framework for assessing the maturity of specific technologies or knowledge areas. The development of BB technologies for fusion energy systems progresses through a series of Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs), each representing a critical step from basic research (TRL 1-3), to technology development demonstration (TRL 4-6), ending at the full-scale deployment (TRL 7-9), see Figure 4: Figure 4: TRL scale  TRLs 1-3 focus on fundamental research - e.g., measuring material properties under neutron irradiation, and conducting small-scale tests on swelling, tritium retention, and helium embrittlement.
 TRLs 4-5 involve validation in laboratory and relevant environments. Key activities include single-effect experiments (e.g., heat transfer, MHD, tritium permeation), small-scale mock-up testing, and early validation of diagnostic tools.
 TRL 6 focuses on subsystem testing under realistic neutron loads, including integrated multi-effect experiments. Facilities like IFMIF-DONES, in particular the use of its medium flux area for breeding blanket module testing, complementary irradiation sources and their combination with non-nuclear facilities will be crucial for achieving the relevant and partially integrated load conditions.
At TRL 6, we stand at the critical boundary between technology development and industrial deployment. Up to this point, efforts in laboratories and research centres are focussed on exploring new concepts, building prototypes, and validating them in controlled or simulated environments. This phase prioritizes innovation, scientific understanding, and technical feasibility, often with limited focus on cost-effectiveness, scalability, or operational constraints. However, as we approach TRL 6, the focus should begin to shift. The technology must now demonstrate its performance in more relevant operational settings, bridging the gap between experimental promise and real-world application. This marks the transition toward deployment, where industry requirements, such as reliability, efficiency, regulatory compliance, and customer acceptance, become dominant concerns.
In essence, TRL 6 defines the tipping point: where the knowledge and possibilities generated through research must begin to transform into practical, market-ready solutions. In this context, to advance with BB technologies, it will be essential to exploit non-nuclear facilities together with fusion-relevant neutron sources that can bring not only a relevant fusion neutron spectrum, but also coolant and tritium carrier fluid conditions and ideally heat flux loading to replicate thermomechanical behaviour of the blanket, which is required at this stage. In this way, one can plan partially integrated experiments so as to gain the needed confidence before getting on board industry and other private actors.
 TRL 7 entails validation of near-final BB technologies under operational conditions, including thermo-mechanical and EM loads. In order to reach such a technology maturation level it is essential to identify the target architecture for the DEMO/FPP machine to properly determine testing requirements and plan the relevant experiments.
 TRL 8 address full system qualification in a Fusion Pilot Plant environment. This includes lifetime testing, supply chain verification, and maintenance procedure qualification.
 TRL 9 should demonstrate the deployment and successful mission operations of the technology at a commercial scale.
As TRLs increase, risks are reduced through structured testing, validation, and the accumulation of performance data. This approach links component testing, design verification, and operational readiness in a data-driven, stepwise progression.
Advancing BB technologies in a coordinated manner is essential to reducing associated risks. This will be achieved through deeper understanding of key technologies, validation of predictive models with experimental results, and demonstration of system performance under fusion-relevant conditions.
Establishing a clear correlation between TRL advancement and risk reduction ensures that BB technologies are rigorously evaluated before deployment in DEMO or future fusion pilot plants – minimizing the likelihood and impact of failure and supporting safety, licensing, and performance objectives.
The qualification of BB technologies demands a tightly integrated approach that combines TRL progression with structured risk management and clearly defined testing requirements.

Indeed, at this level only TBM DT-2 phase (which will likely come too late, at least for our German fusion roadmap) and DONES (assuming that no delays happen in this project) are planned. While higher neutron fluence levels may still require support from DONES, CANSKA can reduce reliance on other facilities for generating critical TRL 6 data.
Its key strength lies in providing a realistic, integrated testing environment for system-level qualification. Delivering 1 dpa/FPY and combining representative operational conditions – such as purge gas, cooling, and, where feasible, heat flux on the first wall – with modular testing blanket units, CANSKA allows the study of component interactions within a complete system unit, yielding meaningful insights that are highly effective for advancing TRL 6.
Beyond TRL6, a VNS or a pilot plant are the only machines than can cover the last stages of pre-commissioning (TRL7-8) before the commercial FoaK at TRL9. However, while VNS could be useful to give some answers at TRL7, it lacks of the representativity of stellarator or laser inertial machines (which are the candidate machines being proposed in the German fusion roadmap) and definitely will not be enough to accomplish the full qualification of the breeding blanket for this kind of machines.
An alternative strategy would be a staged approach towards the FoaK in which industry and research institutions work together in the construction of a machine that can cover TRL7-9 in different phases.
This approach is currently considered by Japan4 and China5 and has been the EU-DEMO strategy until recently. The new fusion roadmap in Japan foresees an ITER-like device with 3 phases, going from 500 MW to 1000 MW fusion power, qualifying in this way the in-vessel components through the different phases. Similarly, China foresees the CFETR machine, going from 200 MW to 1000 MW electric power, also qualifying the in-vessel components on the way to full power operation. In all the cases, closing the risk gap up to TRL6 is of paramount importance before going into the capital-intense TRL7-9.
Discussion and Conclusive remarks During the meeting of the German DEMO Working Group in Greifswald on 30.06.2025, a joint strategy of the German fusion centers for the development of a Breeding Blanket for a fusion reactor was discussed. The starting point is the determination of the Technology Readiness Levels (TRL) for the Breeding Blanket. Currently, this has been identified at TRL 3-4, with nuclear aspects in particular not yet significantly experimentally investigated (only in fission material test reactors to test some functional and structural materials).
During the meeting, a strategy for the qualification of the breeding blanket has been prepared and presented. This strategy begins with the identification of the possible failure modes that the breeding blanket can suffer and the identification and classification of testing needs, which builds the foundation for the technology maturation of this system. The testing needs are classified with increasing level of representativeness of the loading conditions and phenomena combination, climbing in this way the TRL ladder.
Regarding the TRLs, there is agreement that:  The goal of publicly funded programs should be to achieve, at least, TRL 6 in all systems.

 From TRL 7 onwards, industry should step in. In particular, TRL 7-8 involve prototypes in a real operational environment, which in our case would correspond to a DEMO/Pilot Plant, while TRL 9 corresponds to a First-of-a-Kind (FoaK), commercial grade fusion power plant (FPP).
With the original ITER TBM program, TRL 6 (‘Technology demonstrated in relevant environment’) could have been achieved (consistent with the above definition of the role of publicly funded facilities), and, due to the real operational conditions, it would have had an important contribution to TRL 7. However, it is currently unclear whether the TBM program in the new baseline can still deliver this (reduced fluence), and this input will, in any case, come much later than originally anticipated (> 2045).
Therefore, EUROfusion is discussing the construction of a Volumetric Neutron Source (VNS). This would indeed be well-suited to achieve TRL 6 and advance up to TRL 7 (‘System prototype demonstrated in operational environment’). However, TRL 7-8 (where the full qualification of the components before application into a FoaK is achieved) ultimately requires an integrated demonstration in a DEMO/Pilot Plant (with downscaled power with respect to a FoaK FPP). Also, the VNS is based on a tokamak architecture without burning plasma. For this reason, the VNS will not offer the operational conditions of a stellarator or inertial fusion power plant, which are the architecture types in which the German roadmap is focusing on at a public and industrial level. This is also reflected in the fusion start-up ecosystem in Germany. Therefore, the (full) qualification of the breeding blanket for these machine types (achieved at a TRL 8) will not be possible in a VNS, but rather in dedicated stellarator and/or laser inertial fusion pilot plants. In any case, the achievement of TRL 6 by the public research centers is of paramount importance for industry in order to bridge the risk gap before taking further major steps towards a pilot plant to enter the commissioning phase, before full mission deployment in a FoaK FPP.
Here, a critical gap has been identified, in which very few facilities are planned (indeed, only DONES and ITER DT-2 at the moment) that can be used to reach TRL6, from which only DONES can realistically contribute within the timeframe of the current German fusion roadmap, provided the medium flux area is used adequately for blanket qualification.
In view of the unclear final architecture of the fusion reactor (tokamak vs stellarator vs inertial fusion), a more flexible strategy (in the sense that it can be easily adapted to multiple fusion architectures) is envisaged to progress at least towards TRL 6. This would require a combination of multiple facilities.
One of them is DONES, provided it can make use of the Medium Flux Area, making a substantial contribution, especially in terms of neutron fluence relevancy. Another important addition could be an accelerator-driven neutron source, such as the CANSKA proposal at KIT. This facility would implement not only a relevant neutron fluence, but also enable system integration (purge gas, cooling) and represent loading conditions (coolant pressure, and ideally also heat flux at the first wall). These features could realistically contribute to cover testing needs required to reach TRL 6, especially when combined with DONES (providing the additional fluence that CANSKA alone may not be able to achieve).
The SHINE project does not provide the neutron fluence necessary to perform integrated multi-effect experiments required for TRL 6. On the other hand, it is the only concept which provides 14 MeV neutrons from a DT fusion reaction. Consequently, it has the capability to validate the essential functionality of the tritium breeding process in the blanket (combined neutron multiplication and tritium breeding) in a shorter term. Also, such a source also allows for unique investigations of the damage formation from DT-fusion neutrons in plasma facing materials and its consequences for tritium retention, material embrittlement and subsequent damage under plasma impact. Such tests are possible in the High Temperature Material Laboratory at Forschungszentrum Jülich. Given the relatively shorter time for realization of the concept (the DT source is commercially available), the SHINE project could ideally complement the proposed accelerator-driven neutron sources, also providing a valuable risk mitigation.
This discussion indicates that corresponding upgrades to DONES and the construction of a neutron source on the scale of the CANSKA project at KIT, as a multiple-effect, integral blanket qualification platforms, should be strongly supported. The SHINE project, proposed by Forschungszentrum Jülich, should be seen as a significant complement to validate the essential function of the tritium breeding beforehand, with additional important single-effect testing capabilities for the assessment of fuel retention in plasma facing materials under irradiation by DT-fusion neutrons. The combined results from these facilities will provide the required information to industry in terms of performance, safety and reliability of the blanket, necessary for the licensing of a pilot plant, to advance towards the full qualification of the breeding blanket, thus accelerating the German fusion program.
